Reviewer 1
==========

1. Section 3.1 mentions that all three vantage points use the same us-central1-a zone, but Section 4 mentions that "A preliminary comparison across all three geographically distributed vantage points [...]".

2. Section 4.3 upstream bandwidth measurement. The 34.6% packet loss seems very high. How do the authors compute this packet loss? It seems the test sends at a constant 100 Mbps rate, and the packet loss is due to limited available bandwidth.

3. Section 4.5 claims an existing path discrepancy based on a single reported value: 20 vs 18 paths, at "multiple intervals". Can we have more details, as this result carries alone the second contribution of the paper?

4. Though I am not an expert on SCION, could the authors' use of VM as VP to join SCIONLab be the reason for these path churns?

5. Instead of relying on independent tests to measure multipath, couldn't the authors design a real multipath test? This would provide more evidence of a single-path vs multiple-path performance difference.

6. An 8.6-hour path lifetime is more than enough for standard communication.

7. The authors claim that "[Multipath] protocols often assume that a path usable by the sender is equally usable by the receiver for return traffic [...] this assumption does not hold in SCION". It is true that most of the time, we assume path symmetry, but current (multipath) protocols do not entirely rely on this assumption. For example, (Multipath) QUIC performs path probing to ensure that the path is usable in both directions; the path is considered valid only then. Also, I don't fully understand why this "problem" only applies to multipath; this assumption has direct implications for unipath as well.

8. "[Multipath QUIC and likes] implicitly rely on forward-path RTT for timing reverse path acknowledgments." Again, this is not entirely true for Multipath QUIC, where an acknowledgment can be sent on another path from which the data was sent.

9. Figure 8 does not support the claim drawn from it. I am not asserting the claim is false, only that this evidence does not establish it. The "boxes" are flat and centered near 0, indicating no real difference between a single path and multiple paths. The outliers do not indicate a tendency in the "tail" distribution. Use another visualization (e.g., a CDF) and provide a statistical test to determine whether there is a significant difference between a single path and multiple paths. How do you compute the metric from the figure? What does it represent? How does it translate from RTT+jitter+losses?

10. The authors limit path analysis to 15 randomly picked paths, and the multipath study to 2 simultaneous paths. What are the implications of selecting these paths randomly?

11. The mp-prober uses totally disjoint paths. While this is sound, isn't it too strong a hypothesis? Multipath protocols in BGP-based networks expose per-path differences, even when the paths used in the backbone are identical.

12. Figure 2: The addition and removal of paths seem correlated, more like it is a path change rather than distinct removal and addition. In that regard, how do these paths change? Even though SCION requires the explicit full path, it would be interesting to consider where these paths change. Moreover, why do we have so many changes to the path?

13. Section 4.2. The reported RTT - more than 400 ms - seems gigantic. Even though SCION runs atop the existing Internet architecture, it seems relatively large. Do the authors know why?

14. Figure 5 shows an event that affects the RTT. The authors do not know the reason for this significant change; did they try to ask SCION's maintainers?

15. Section 4.3 reports a single metric of 87.09 Mbps. Is it consistent over the 4-week study period? The authors talk about server-to-client and client-to-server asymmetry. If I remember correctly, these measurements are performed between the vantage points. As a result, I don't clearly understand why there is a client-server pattern.

Additional comments:

16. The figures could be improved. At least they should be the same size (e.g., title font size), as in Figures 3 and 4. All figures should be vectorized, not PNG/JPEG.

17. At the very end of Section 2.1, the last sentence is repeated twice.
Response: We thank the reviewer for catching this. The duplicated sentence at the end of Section 2.1 was an editing artifact and has been removed.

18. Avoid using the term MPQUIC for Multipath QUIC. Though MPTCP is used for Multipath TCP, this is not (anymore) the case for QUIC.

19. Reference 26 has a placeholder name.


Reviewer 2
==========

1. The motivation for path selection is compelling and helps frame the results on path discrepancy and multipath performance. It may help to briefly connect path churn to this use case as well, so readers can better understand why path dynamics are especially important. E.g., the insight into how churn can affect path orchestration in the paper's conclusions section is very relevant.

2. On page 4, the sentence “For a comprehensive technical specification, see the SCION book [15] and prior architectural analyses [6].” appears twice.
Response: We thank the reviewer for catching this. The duplicated sentence at the end of Section 2.1 was an editing artifact and has been removed.

3. The paper currently has relatively few figures. Adding a simple figure of the SCION architecture could make Section 2.1 even easier to follow.

4. For the phrase “After an initial stabilization window, ...”, a short explanation of how this window was chosen and why those data points were excluded would improve reproducibility.

5. Figure 1 is a little hard to read in its current form. Improving resolution and expanding the caption would likely help readers interpret it more confidently.

6. For “by transmitting a fixed number of probe packets per path,” it would be helpful to state the exact number of packets and the rationale for that choice.

7. Could you clarify how long each bandwidth test was run?

8. The results section contains several interesting findings. It may become even stronger with a bit more discussion of possible root causes behind the observed trends.

9. On path availability, did you look into the composition of dynamic paths to identify common structural patterns? Also, in Figure 2, the added and removed path counts appear close together; a brief explanation would be helpful.

10. Figure 4 uses fairly wide histogram bins. If possible, would narrower bins reveal additional detail, especially for short-lived paths?

11. Figure 6 shows an interesting RTT effect. It may help to discuss how likely random path selection is in practice at SCION endpoints. A comparison of average RTT versus minimum RTT might also highlight the effect of path choice more clearly.

12. Section 4.3 is currently brief. A time-series view might help readers understand temporal behavior. Also, since 34.6% packet loss is quite high, it would be helpful to explain how 65 Mbps throughput is still achieved under those conditions.

13. The sentence “This persistent upstream degradation points to systemic bottlenecks or restrictive forwarding policies within the SCIONLab testbed infrastructure.” reads a bit abruptly; a small wording revision could improve flow.

14. The point about exponential moving averages becoming stale under high path churn is very insightful. It might be valuable to introduce or emphasize this idea earlier, for example, in Section 4.1.

15. "invalidates the core assumption of bidirectional symmetry that underlies conventional network management and transport design." Can you provide a reference to support this statement?

Reviewer 1, Comment 3
We have expanded Section 4.5 with aggregate statistics computed over the full stable measurement window. Path-set sizes differed between the two directions in 31.6% of intervals; the mean absolute difference was 0.63 paths (median 0); the largest observed difference was 8 paths. These results confirm that the asymmetry is systematic rather than an isolated observation. The original 20-versus-18 example is retained as a concrete illustration.

Reviewer 1, Comment 2
The 34.6% upstream loss is the average loss ratio reported by scion bwtestclient while the client was sending at the requested 100 Mbps tier. The high loss occurs because the available upstream capacity on the SCIONLab paths was lower than the offered load; packets in excess of the available bandwidth were dropped. It is therefore a direct consequence of the directional capacity limit rather than an independent random-loss process.

Reviewer 1, Comment 15 and Reviewer 2, Comment 12
The reported downstream (87.09 Mbps) and upstream (65.4 Mbps) averages were stable across the 27-day measurement window. The client-to-server / server-to-client terminology reflects the roles assigned by the scion bwtestclient tool in each directed test; the client is our vantage point and the server is a SCIONLab bandwidth-test server (18-ffaa:0:1201 or 19-ffaa:0:1303). The 65.4 Mbps upstream throughput is the direct result of the 34.6% loss under a 100 Mbps offered load (100 × (1 − 0.346) ≈ 65.4). We have added these clarifications (and a time-series view) to Section 4.3.

Reviewer 1, Comments 4 and 12 and Reviewer 2, Comment 9
We analysed the path fingerprints of all six directed vantage-point series over the stable window. The additions and removals in Figure 3 (Figure 2 in the original submission) are correlated, but they are rarely path replacements (same endpoints, different intermediate segments): only 33 of 1,126 change events (2.9%) contain an addition and a removal in the same interval, and 99.6% of the added paths (3,327 of 3,339) had already been alive earlier in the window. Each directed pair lists only 18-24 distinct paths over the 27 days; the churn is mostly the same paths being withdrawn and restored, either as the whole path set reappearing after an interval in which scion showpaths returned no viable path (77% and 88% of the additions toward 17-ffaa:1:11e4 and 18-ffaa:1:11e5 from the representative vantage point) or as single-interval timeouts of individual paths (97% of 979 timeout episodes). The only change in path-set composition is on 2 August 2025 at 11:00, when the 9-11 paths via the ISD 16 core AS 16-ffaa:0:1001 are no longer listed (four paths via 20-ffaa:0:1401 appear at the same time for one pair); otherwise paths of a pair share the same first and last three ASes and differ only in the core transit segment and the interfaces used. The observed churn appears in the path sets returned by the SCION control-plane API at all three vantage points, and the 127-interval outage of 17-ffaa:1:11e4 is seen identically by all of them, so it is not specific to one measurement VM; however, most short-lived events do not coincide across vantage points, so we cannot exclude a contribution from the local host or SCION daemon. We have added this analysis to Section 4.1.

Reviewer 1, Comment 6
We agree that an average path lifetime of 8.6–15.7 hours is adequate for typical short-lived transfers. We have clarified in the Discussion that the same lifetime is nevertheless short for long-lived connections, for path-scoring services that rely on historical averages, and for automated SLA verification. In those settings the measured churn rate sets the required update frequency.

Reviewer 1, Comments 7 and 8 and Reviewer 2, Comment 15
We have revised the discussion of path symmetry to acknowledge that modern multipath protocols such as Multipath QUIC perform bidirectional path validation and can send acknowledgments on any validated path. We also note that the directional discrepancy we measured affects unipath protocols as well. A reference supporting the traditional symmetry assumption has been added.

Reviewer 1, Comment 9
We have replaced the original boxplot with a CDF of the per-path differences and added a paired statistical test. The median difference in RTT is -0.05 ms (Wilcoxon p = 0.78); the corresponding values for jitter and loss are +0.06 ms (p < 10^-6 over 5,792 probe pairs, but p = 0.07 over the 44 per-path medians; effect size r = 0.08) and 0 percentage points (p = 3x10^-6 over probe pairs, r = 0.23; the median loss difference is zero on every path, so no per-path test is possible). The revised figure and text in Section 4.4 now present the strength of the evidence more accurately. In particular, we no longer claim that every latency-sensitive metric degrades: typical per-path RTT is unchanged under multipath, and we report only a modest increase in the upper tail of the jitter distribution and a small excess of loss events. Section 4.4 now also explains how the metric is computed (MP minus SP over matched probe pairs of the same path, probes about seven minutes apart, 15 pings each, jitter taken as the mdev reported by scion ping), and states that the MP paths are drawn at random rather than guaranteed to be disjoint. The other two vantage points show the same qualitative picture. We also aligned the rest of the manuscript (abstract, contributions list, Discussion, and Conclusion) with these statistical results.

Reviewer 1, Comments 10 and 11 and Reviewer 2, Comment 11
We have clarified the sampling methodology in Section 3.2. Up to 15 paths were chosen uniformly at random to characterize the available path set without selection bias, and concurrent tests were limited to 2–3 paths to limit load on the shared testbed. The paths were not required to be strictly disjoint. We also note that production endpoints may preferentially select lower-RTT paths; the random-sampling results therefore represent a conservative baseline rather than an optimized selection policy.
